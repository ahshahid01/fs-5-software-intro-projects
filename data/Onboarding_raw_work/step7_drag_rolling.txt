Step 7 - Drag + Rolling Resistance from the Coastdown Data

Task (projects.md): use the Step 6 coast down data to find drag (Cd*A)
and rolling resistance (Crr), by fitting a straight line to dv/dt vs v^2.

   dv/dt = -((.5 * p * Cd * A) / m_eff) * v^2 - (Crr * m * g) / m_eff
           \_________ slope __________/          \____ intercept ____/

   .5 * p * Cd * A = -slope * m_eff
   Crr             = -intercept * m_eff / (m * g)

Constants: air density p = 1.225 kg/m^3, car mass m = 221.4 kg,
effective mass m_eff = 244.08 kg, g = 9.81 m/s^2

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Result
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
   slope           = -0.001538  (+/- 0.001854)
   intercept       = -0.2661 m/s^2  (+/- 0.1089)

   .5 * p * Cd * A = 0.375 kg/m
   Cd * A          = 0.61 m^2     (1 std. error range: about -0.13 to 1.35)
   Crr             = 0.030        (1 std. error range: about 0.018 to 0.042)

   BOTTOM LINE: Crr is roughly known (~0.02 - 0.04). Cd*A is NOT pinned
   down by this data - the uncertainty in the slope is bigger than the
   slope itself, so the data can't rule out zero drag (or even negative,
   which is impossible). 0.61 m^2 is the best-fit value, not a measured
   one.

   Plots: step7_dvdt_vs_v2.png (all points + fit)
          step7_dvdt_vs_v2_zoom.png (y from -1 to 0.5, shows the fit line
          and the coast averages)

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Method (Onboarding.py, Step 7)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
   1. Data: the 4 clean coasts from Step 6 (coast_segments), 3,259 rows,
      all >= 5 m/s.
   2. dv/dt from CONSECUTIVE readings, as projects.md says:
      speed.diff() / time.diff(), done inside each coast separately, so
      dv/dt never jumps from the end of one coast to the start of the next.
      Divided by the real time step, since the segments have small holes
      (largest 0.083 s) where dips under 5 m/s were removed.
   3. Dropped the first row of each coast (no previous reading).
      3,255 points left.
   4. v^2 = speed at the same row, squared.
   5. np.polyfit(v^2, dv/dt, 1) for slope and intercept, then the formulas
      above.

   Decision: kept consecutive readings even though it's very noisy (see
   below), to follow the provided instructions.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Why the data is so noisy
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
   - The speed sensor updates only every ~2nd row; forward-fill copies the
     old value in between. 58% of the dv/dt values are exactly 0, and the
     whole change lands in the next 0.012 s step.
   - The sensor reports speed in small fixed steps, so dv/dt comes out in
     horizontal stripes (0, about +/-0.5, +/-2, +/-4 ... m/s^2).
   - Result: dv/dt spreads about +/-8 m/s^2 (outliers -13 to +19), while
     the real slowdown is only ~0.35 m/s^2. The line explains almost none
     of the scatter (R^2 = 0.0002).
   - The scatter does average out: mean dv/dt over all points is
     -0.349 m/s^2, matching each coast's overall slowdown. So the fit is
     centered on the right values - it just can't be very precise.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Cross-checks
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
   Coast averages (overall slowdown = (last speed - first speed) / time):

     coast               avg v^2     avg dv/dt
     24.63 - 38.77 s      57.1       -0.358 m/s^2
     51.80 - 61.22 s      41.8       -0.334 m/s^2
     99.33 - 103.03 s     98.0       -0.451 m/s^2
    103.83 - 115.46 s     45.9       -0.317 m/s^2

   These sit close to the fitted line (zoom plot), and the fastest coast
   slows down the most, which is what drag should do. Only 4 points
   though, and only one of them is fast.

   Different dv/dt window (0.5 s, .diff(42), same as Step 5):
     Cd*A = 1.19 m^2, Crr = 0.022
   Both inside the uncertainty ranges above, but Cd*A doubles, which shows
   how loosely the data sets the drag value.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Why drag is hard to measure here
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
   The coasts only cover 5 - 10.7 m/s (18 - 39 km/h). With the best-fit
   values:
     at  5.0 m/s: drag ~  9 N, rolling ~ 65 N
     at 10.7 m/s: drag ~ 43 N, rolling ~ 65 N
   Rolling resistance dominates the whole range. Drag only changes the
   slowdown by ~0.14 m/s^2 between the slowest and fastest points, which
   is tiny next to the +/-8 m/s^2 scatter. Faster and longer coasts would
   be needed to measure drag properly.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Are the numbers plausible?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
   - Cd*A ~ 0.6 m^2 is a reasonable size for a car with no wings, but it
     is not reliable (see above).
   - Crr ~ 0.03 is about 2x what tires alone usually give (~0.01 - 0.015).
     Expected to be high here: "rolling resistance" in this fit includes
     everything that doesn't depend on v^2 - chain and bearing friction,
     motor drag while freewheeling, and any slope.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Other caveats
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
   - Slope: the coasts aren't balanced by direction (GPS longitude):
     ~29 s heading east (24.63-38.77, 99.33-103.03, 103.83-115.46) vs
     ~9 s heading west (51.80-61.22). So a hill does not cancel out. The
     west coast slows about the same as the east ones at similar speed,
     which suggests the slope is small, but it isn't proven (GPS altitude
     is too coarse to measure it).
   - Speed is wheel speed from motor RPM. Fine while coasting (no
     throttle, so no wheelspin).
   - m_eff (244.08 kg) and m (221.4 kg) are taken from projects.md as is.
